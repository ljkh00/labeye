PROBLEM STATEMENT
01 · NO DEDICATED LABComputer science students at UOW Malaysia lack a production-grade cybersecurity and networking laboratory. Existing options are too costly to licence, too limited in realism, or unsafe for isolated practice.
02 · SKILLS GAPIndustry expects topologies, firewall policy, routing protocols, and event response. Without a structured lab, graduates cannot demonstrate those competencies on real appliances.
03 · UNUSED HARDWARELab D Acer and Dell hardware remains underutilised. Open-source virtualisation can reuse it, cut recurring licences, and keep the platform under institutional control.
PROBLEM SOLUTION

Build an on-premises Lab D cluster, not a cloud or mobile lab. Acer nodes host concurrent student VMs. The Dell node hosts shared services. IEEE 802.1Q VLANs and Opnsense-main isolate the campus LAN from the exercise fabric.

Students sit at Lenovo PCs, open LabEye (teammate portal), and configure a four-guest overlay: student OPNsense, VyOS, Ubuntu, Windows — without hypervisor administrator rights. Staff open ntopng, d3.js (Cluster Mesh), Wazuh, and Proxmox on VLAN 900. Tokens stay in Express on guest d3.js at 172.29.0.51. Grafana, Netdata, and pfSense are not used.

RESEARCH QUESTIONS
  1. How can existing Lab D hardware be repurposed into a scalable, multi-node virtualised cybersecurity and networking education platform using open-source tools?
  2. To what extent does a virtualised lab using OPNsense and VyOS provide a more realistic learning experience than Cisco Packet Tracer or hardware?
  3. Can lecturers observe live traffic and security events on ntopng, d3.js (Cluster Mesh), and Wazuh during a class, without placing those dashboards on the student path?
  4. What guided networking exercises work at progressively higher skill levels?
  5. How usable is the platform for students with mixed prior experience, as assessed through UAT?
RESEARCH OBJECTIVES
  1. Design and deploy a Proxmox VE cluster on Lab D hardware, capable of hosting concurrent student virtual machine sessions.
  2. Provide realistic student networking workloads using OPNsense and VyOS from classroom PCs, without granting hypervisor administrator rights.
  3. Provide lecturer-facing observability of laboratory traffic and security events using ntopng, d3.js (Cluster Mesh), and Wazuh.
  4. Develop guided lab exercises covering core networking topics at Basic, Intermediate, and Advanced difficulty levels.
  5. Evaluate usability and educational difference through user acceptance testing with student participants.
  6. Produce technical documentation: setup notes, lab manuals, physical and logical topology diagrams, and the final report.

EVALUATION RESULTS
GroupnWhat was testedOutcome
Lecturers3Isolation, ntopng, Cluster Mesh, Wazuh, Proxmox / CephLecturers confirmed that VLAN isolation holds, that ntopng and Cluster Mesh show live traffic, that Wazuh records events, and that Proxmox and Ceph can provision, snapshot, and restore guests on the live cluster.
Students10 consentedLabEye Networking sitting + UAT formStudents used LabEye to reach the virtual networking lab. Most found the virtual environment closer to real devices than Cisco Packet Tracer, with real interfaces and commands, though Packet Tracer stayed easier to start and better for drawing any topology. Compared with physical hardware, they said the virtual lab is more standardised, cheaper to run, and avoids hardware-failure problems, while a real rack still wins for physical ports and specialised components.

Versus Cisco Packet Tracer, students described Lab D as more realistic and better for learning how data actually moves, not as easier. Versus hardware, they valued standardisation and less setup hassle, but still wanted physical ports for some troubleshooting. Packet Tracer remains useful for sketching a topology; the virtual lab is where they configure real firewall and router interfaces.
